Comparing how fast two Indian districts are growing is more frustrating than it ought to be. 

While India's statistical machinery does produce a staggering quantity of data, it is rarely cooperative. It is often outdated, available at inconvenient resolutions, and occasionally simply absent. We know relatively little about how economic activity changes from one district to another in real time. We know even less about something as fundamental as the stock of physical capital. 

There is no official district-level measure of how much has been built, or how quickly the built up environment is changing. Yet infrastructure, industrial concentration, and urban growth---the very indicators that matter most for public policy---are uncooperatively determined sub state level. Aggregated, lagged statistics hide much of the story.

Satellite data offers a solution to this problem. 

Every few days, satellites record the changing surface of the Earth. Buildings appear, urban centres expand. Industrial enclaves emerge. After sunset, settlements brighten or dim as economic activity ebbs and flows. None of this was designed as an economic indicator, but together they reveal patterns of economic development that were previously impossible to observe.

Unfortunately, working with satellite data is its own can of worms. Processing the raw image data available in esoteric formats, normalizing and bias correcting due to myriad factors; anyone hoping for a drop in proxy for economic activity will soon find themselves in need of not only a crash course in satellite imagery, but ideally a supercomputer to process it.

That is what our new processed district level dataset for India draws on: two processed, ready-to-use satellite datasets (and the pipelines that created them), tracked separately, that between them provide a bird's eye view of a district's capital and economic activity.

The first is annual building volume, taken from Google's Open Buildings 2.5D Temporal dataset. Built from Sentinel-2 imagery at an effective resolution of 4m, it estimates not just where buildings stand but how tall they are, providing a proxy for accumulated physical capital, year by year, from 2016 to 2023. The raw data is resampled at a 100m resolution (in the interest of quicker processing, which is plenty for district-level analyses), and intelligently chunked to avoid rate limiting. Each district-year row provides the a sum of building footprints in a district-year, average height per building, and total volume (which is the product of the previous two measures).  

As construction is slow and cumulative, this data moves gradually; it reads less like a snapshot of current activity than a record of what has already been invested in the housing, industry and commercial space. The underlying data is presented here as published, without a separate cleaning pass, and the 2022 estimates in particular look odd enough to warrant a second look. While this is a measure that should ideally show a monotonic increase, 2022 shows an unexpected sharp fall in building volume.

The second is nighttime lights, from the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS), available monthly since 2014 at a 500m resolution. VIIRS provides both average radiance, as well as a count of cloud free images per pixel/month, and is notoriously difficult to work with. Bloom, background noise, outliers (from, say, flaring gas), as well as seasonal attenuation are all well documented problems. Seasonal attenuation is particularly of note, as the increased cloud cover and paucity of cloud free images tends to bias radiance downwards in cloudy months. Indeed, there's a marked and poorly explained correlation between cloud-free images and radiance, which requires significant bias-correction for an issue new users might be wholly unaware of. We take the raw data, batch process them using the PSTT2021 pipeline in `NighttimeLights.jl` (which corrects a majority of the issues), and aggregate them at a district level. Each district-year-month row provides the sum of radiance, mean radiance per pixel, and pixel count for each district-month-year.


Lights respond much more quickly than buildings. They brighten as neighbourhoods become more active and dim when activity slows. If buildings measure accumulated capital, nighttime lights measure an economy in motion. One caveat is worth flagging: as household and street lighting shifts to LEDs, measured radiance can fall even where activity is rising, because LEDs emit less in the band VIIRS is sensitive to. A dimmer district is not necessarily a slower one.

Read on their own, each answers a straightforward question. Read together, they answer a more useful one. A district where both building volume and lights are climbing is likely in the midst of genuine, sustained growth. Where construction is rising but lights remain flat, new capacity is being built ahead of actual use. Possibly a reflection of the shrewd foresight of planners anticipating imminent development, or, at the other extreme, a ghost town left behind by overestimating future demand. Conversely, where lights rise with little new construction, the signal points toward existing capital being used more intensively rather than the district expanding outward, potentially reflecting constrained resources, limited confidence in long term growth, or a deliberate choice to extract more from what is already there.

We built this dataset to make these kinds of questions easier to ask. We built it at a scale India's official statistics don't reach: the district, updated annually or monthly instead of once a decade. It should be useful to researchers working on regional inequality, urbanisation and the geography of growth, and to anyone in government trying to keep track of places where the usual numbers arrive too late (if at all).

The processing pipeline is public and runs end to end in two colab notebooks, one for each dataset, with the cleaned outputs also available as CSVs. Swap the geography to point it at a different country or a different administrative boundary, and the pipelines will cooperate. Economic development leaves visible traces on the landscape long before it appears in statistical data. Satellites make those traces visible. Our hope is that this dataset makes them usable.
